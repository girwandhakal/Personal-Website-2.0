Making a cart review fast enough for checkout

I worked on a browser extension that gives shoppers a quick second look at their cart. The catch was making the review fast enough to be worth the interruption.

Where a second look could help

We built WorthIt for the fintech track at Hacklanta 2026. By the time someone reaches checkout on Amazon or Walmart, a recent purchase or a cheaper store-brand option can be easy to miss. We wanted to surface those details while the shopper could still do something about them.

The extension can pause the first checkout click once and flag up to three items. The shopper can continue right away and stays in charge of the cart and checkout.

How we built the review

Normalize retailer data

Amazon and Walmart lay out their carts differently. We used retailer adapters to turn those rows into the same item format and accounted for single-page navigation so the review activates on the cart route.

Ask before assuming

A familiar item might be a duplicate, a replacement, or a gift. The review asks when it needs that context. It also checks supported store-brand swaps, novelty items, and a fixed set of food-label concerns.

Respond before enrichment finishes

Waiting for every product detail made the first review take about four seconds in testing. We let the review open while those details loaded, which brought the first response down to 0.27 seconds, a 93% reduction. Walmart product-label facts are cached locally for repeat visits.

Keep history local

Purchase history stays in IndexedDB. The optional Gmail import uses read-only access, parses receipts locally, and lets the shopper review records before saving them. We discard email bodies and keep the retailer scripts away from payment fields, bank accounts, and general browsing history.

What we kept out of the checkout flow

We put the quick recap at checkout and moved item-by-item findings and sources into a separate view. That gave shoppers a way to check the reasoning without reading it all before continuing.

An optional chart illustrates what potential savings could become under hypothetical assumptions, without personal account data. The optional AI copy pass sees placeholders for names, prices, and history. The assessment keeps the underlying facts in structured fields.

Running the assessment locally meant we could build the core flow without a backend. It also ties history to the device. Public distribution and Gmail OAuth verification are separate steps from running the unpacked hackathon build.

What I'd show while details load

We got the full review loop working and measured the time to the first response. Browser fixtures and automated tests cover repeatable retailer interactions. Live retailer pages and Gmail still need separate checks.

I came away thinking differently about what has to finish before a screen can open. At checkout I'd show what we already know, leave pending facts visibly pending, and let the shopper decide how long to stay.

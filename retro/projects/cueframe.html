Why I built Cueframe

A deck gives me the order of a talk. I still have to figure out how to say it and keep my place once I'm presenting. I built Cueframe so I could start with a deck, edit the draft slide by slide, then check how my timing held up afterward.

The presenter can change the wording and set the pace. The audience sees the slides while the speaker keeps the script and cues in a separate window.

From slide import to the presenter window

Keep a local working copy

I keep projects, slide images, script versions, and sessions in IndexedDB. I render PDFs with pdf.js and read PowerPoint files from their XML. The PowerPoint preview is simplified, so I'd export to PDF if I needed the slide to look exactly right.

Bound the AI pipeline

Analysis starts during import. I use the deck context and outline to guide the draft, with a local planner that assigns a word budget based on the time available and the speaker's pace. The writer handles four slides per request, with up to three requests running at once.

Validate before saving

A good paragraph on the wrong slide is still a bad draft. I check the slide IDs for omissions, duplicates, and IDs from another deck, and save the draft once every slide passes. A fingerprint catches edits made during generation. Earlier versions stay available, and the Lexical editor lets the presenter accept or discard a proposed rewrite.

Separate presenter and audience

The presenter window scrolls at the speaker's words per minute and has slide timers, a reading line, and a countdown they can cancel. I send slide state through BroadcastChannel and keep script text and cues in the presenter window.

Make sync optional

I made Firestore and Storage sync optional. Each device remembers where it left off, and an IndexedDB outbox retries writes that haven't been confirmed. I reserve the maximum token cost of each request before it runs so two calls can't spend the same allowance.

What the slower writer got right

I compared drafts on held-out decks with the inputs fixed and the order swapped in blind comparisons. The version that reviewed and repaired drafts won both orders in 13 of 15 completed pairs. Its source check rejected three of 18 attempts, it used about twice as many generation tokens, and median latency went from 8.859 to 26.76 seconds on three-slide fixtures.

User feedback gave me a better question to ask. People were running into awkward paraphrases and summaries that didn't sound spoken. I took out the routine review and repair passes and worked on the writing guide and local checks. The earlier comparison measures the heavier experiment. The current pipeline would need its own evaluation.

I use a deterministic demo provider to run unit, API, security-rule, and browser tests without spending model credits. One browser test checks that the audience window stays free of private script text. I evaluate writing quality separately.

What I'd test with presenters next

The workspace runs from import through timing review, with optional AI and cloud sync. I'm aiming for a hosted, invite-only pilot.

I used to look at the win count first. Now I'd listen to people read the script before deciding what to measure. A comparison can tell me which draft wins and still leave me with the wrong default.
